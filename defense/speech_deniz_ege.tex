% ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\slidehdr{Slide 2 \,\textbullet\, Flowchart: how one kit order is handled}{\(\approx\)55 s}
\begin{say}
``This one diagram is the entire pipeline. A kit order arrives, and these orders do
not come one at a time, they arrive in batches, exactly the way the real dispatch log
shows. For every material in that kit, the model looks up where it is stored, and that
location is decided by the slotting policy we are testing. Then one of three things
happens. If the material is in the Kardex, an automated carousel brings it. If it sits
low and within hand reach, an operator simply walks over and picks it. If it is up
high, the operator has to wait for a reach truck. The operator assembles the full kit,
walks it to the production line, and we record the KPIs: lead time, waiting time,
walking distance, and utilization. Then the next order. Six policies, twenty
independent runs each.''
\end{say}
\cue{Click to the SimPy logic slide.}

% ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\slidehdr{Slide 3 \,\textbullet\, SimPy logic, distribution-driven}{\(\approx\)80 s}
\begin{say}
``Now the part that makes this credible. The model is distribution-driven, it is not a
replay. I did not record history and play it back. I fit statistical distributions from
four months of the dispatch log, more than a hundred and sixty thousand rows, and I
sample from them. That distinction is the whole reason this works: because the demand is
generated and not fixed, I can move materials to new locations and re-run the same
warehouse under a different policy, and the comparison stays fair.

A few numbers behind it. The arrivals follow the real batch pattern, and across the full
twenty-run set the model lands at about four hundred orders a day, within roughly one
percent of the real average of three hundred ninety-six. Pick times are not constant,
they are lognormal, and the spread comes straight from a video time study of the F400
line, more than two thousand timed micro-events, through this formula:
\(\sigma=\sqrt{\ln\!\left(1+\mathrm{CV}^{2}\right)}\). So the variability is measured,
not invented. The model also holds the real fleet: eight operators, seven reach trucks,
and four Kardex units, and any rack position can be picked by only one person at a time.
And to keep the comparison airtight, every policy faces the exact same random demand,
using common random numbers.''
\end{say}

\par\smallskip
{\color{navy}\bfseries If the jury asks (sim part)}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \emph{Replay or distribution-driven?} ``Distribution-driven. I sample from
        fitted distributions, which is exactly what lets me re-slot and re-run
        counterfactually.''
  \item \emph{Chi-square is significant, is the model wrong?} ``No. It is significant
        because the model uses its own routing, not a bin-for-bin replay. Cramer's V is
        0.22, small to moderate. It is an internal-consistency check.''
  \item \emph{RT utilization 1.3\% seems too low.} ``That is picking workload only.
        Replenishment is excluded equally for all six policies, so the comparison is
        fair; live total RT load would be higher.''
  \item \emph{F400 timing extrapolated to nine families?} ``Bounded by a plus-minus
        twenty percent sensitivity sweep. Line-aware stays first under every swing.''
\end{itemize}
